## Why this framing works — the psychology to preserve

The framing makes *not subscribing* the risky-looking choice. A production
services CEO reading:

> "We watch seven studio job boards and five state incentive programs
> continuously, so your salespeople call the moment money moves."

...doesn't think "I could build that." He thinks **"my competitor might
already get this."** Fear of missing the lead beats pride of DIY every time.

That one framing is simultaneously:
- **The launch argument** — subscribe now because money is moving now.
- **The price-justification argument** — you're not paying for a newsletter,
  you're paying for the machine that never stops watching.
- **The renewal argument** — cancel, and the watching stops while your
  competitor's doesn't.

## The honest version of the moat (background, so the copy stays true)

Nothing we do is secret or impossible to replicate — a determined engineer
could rebuild any piece. What can't be cheaply replicated:

1. **Accumulated gotcha knowledge** — job boards that report fake counts when
   a search is empty, stories that reappear on re-crawls, state sites that
   block bots. All of it is already encoded in our pipeline; a DIYer discovers
   these failures weeks in, silently.
2. **The maintenance treadmill** — sources change constantly; our system
   notices when a source quietly breaks. A DIY script rots in silence.
3. **The economics** — for anyone whose job is selling to Hollywood buyers,
   spending engineer-months rebuilding this is a bad trade. The moat isn't
   secrecy; it's that DIY is irrational.

It's the Bloomberg moat: nothing they do is magic, nobody sane rebuilds it.
