# Book test: three pattern questions against California's film-incentive archive

**For:** Karilyn · **Date:** August 2, 2026 · **Verdict up front: the book has a data spine.** All three questions produced something a general reader would find surprising, and every number below traces to a public record.

## What I did

The California Film Commission publishes its complete approved-projects list — every project, company, credit amount, and job count since the program began. The full archive is 907 approved projects across 17 fiscal years (2009–10 through 2025–26). I pulled the whole table and ran three pattern questions against it, then checked the results against the Legislative Analyst's Office program review and the Governor's 2025 expansion announcement.

**Sources for every number below:**
- CA Film Commission approved projects list (all rounds, fetched Aug 2, 2026): https://film.ca.gov/film-and-television-tax-credit-program-approved-projects-list/
- CA Legislative Analyst's Office, "The 2023-24 Budget: California's Film Tax Credit": https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/4713
- Governor's office, July 2, 2025 expansion announcement: https://www.gov.ca.gov/2025/07/02/governor-newsom-marks-historic-expansion-of-californias-film-and-television-tax-credit-program-announces-16-new-projects-to-film-in-the-golden-state/

## Question 1: Who actually gets the money — and can a taxpayer even tell?

**Finding: 86% of the company names on the list appear exactly once — but those single-appearance companies collected only 26% of the dollars.** 544 distinct company names; 466 appear once; the one-timers took $1.36B of the $5.20B total allocated across the program's life.

The inversion is the story: the list *looks* like a crowd of small independent producers, but nearly three-quarters of the money went to repeat players — and the biggest repeat players are sometimes invisible. Two examples:

- **Twentieth Century Fox is the program's largest recipient: 54 projects, $621.6M** — about 12% of everything California has ever allocated, under one studio's name.
- **The 3rd-largest recipient is "Cooler Waters Productions, LLC" — $183.0M across 10 projects.** No taxpayer scanning the list would know who that is. The projects tell you: *Euphoria*, *Perry Mason*, *Winning Time*, *In Treatment*, *The Comeback*, *I Love LA* — it's the LLC through which HBO's flagship California shows collect their credits. **This is the chapter-one anecdote.** The state's own transparency page discloses a name that conceals more than it reveals — and connecting shell names to studios is exactly the "read the public record smarter" method the book teaches. (For the book: verify the HBO link via the shows' own credits/trade coverage before printing; the state list itself only gives the LLC name and titles.)

**Why it surprised me:** I expected single-project LLCs to be where the money hid. It's the opposite — the money is concentrated, and the concealment is in the *naming*, not the fragmentation.

## Question 2: Is the indie set-aside real or decorative?

**Finding: for most of a decade, independents got less than their legal reservation.** The statute reserved 5% of credits for independent films under Program 2.0 (2015–2020) and 8% under Program 3.0 (2020–2025) (source: LAO report, Figure 3). The list shows independents' actual share of allocated dollars:

| Period | Indie share of allocated $ |
|---|---|
| 2015–16 through 2019–20 (Program 2.0, 5% set-aside) | 4.1%–6.6%, trending down each year |
| 2020–21 (Program 3.0, 8% set-aside) | **2.7%** — the program's all-time low |
| 2021–22 through 2024–25 | 8.1%–12.1% (recovered to roughly the set-aside) |
| 2025–26 (expanded program) | 12.3% of dollars — and 94 of 175 projects |

This confirms the book's "trough" thesis with receipts: the trough was legally open, but in the years the majors were feasting hardest (2017–2021), indies' actual share sat at or below the carve-out — bottoming out during COVID at 2.7%. Then the 2025 expansion produced an indie stampede: more independent projects approved in 2025–26 alone (94) than in the previous seven fiscal years combined. (Caveat for print: set-asides are enforced per allocation round against reserved pools, so "share of the year's dollars" is an outcome measure, not proof any round violated the reservation — the honest framing is "the reservation existed; the money still didn't flow.")

## Question 3: How did the awards themselves change as the bidding war escalated?

Three escalations, all from the same table:

1. **Average award: $2.1M (2009–10) → $9.0M (2025–26).** Annual totals: $83M in year one → $1.58B allocated in FY 2025–26 after the cap more than doubled from $330M to $750M/yr (source: Governor's office, July 2025). The largest single award in program history — **$48.0M, allocated March 9, 2026 to "Unt. DET Project 13"** — appeared on the state's list under only that code name. Its identity did not come from the public record at all: trade press revealed it nearly two months later as *Tracker*, the CBS drama relocating from Vancouver for season 4 (Deadline, May 4, 2026: https://deadline.com/2026/05/tracker-relocating-los-angeles-season-4-tax-credit-1236879144/). Bonus concealment: the recipient studio is Disney's 20th Television, but the taxpayer of record on the list is "Twentieth Century Fox Film Corporation" — a Disney studio, making a CBS show, filed under a pre-merger Fox name.
2. **Credit dollars per person hired roughly tripled: ~$11,100 per cast+crew hire (2009–12) → ~$35,400 (2023–26)** — the state pays three times as much per disclosed hire as it did at the start. (Directional, not causal: production cost inflation and the shift toward big-budget series both feed this; the book should present it as "the price of a job tripled on paper" and let the LAO's skepticism about returns carry the argument.)
3. **Secrecy grew with the money: zero "Untitled" projects on the 2015–16 or 2019–20 lists; 34 of 175 in 2025–26.** As awards got bigger, the public list got vaguer.

## Addendum (Aug 2, 2026): two follow-up finds with receipts

- **The $48M mystery is solved — by the trades, not the state.** See Question 3 above: "Unt. DET Project 13" is *Tracker* (Deadline, May 4, 2026). The book point is the disclosure gap itself: the state's transparency list carried only a code name at award time.
- **The "pay twice" hook has a famous face.** *Leave the World Behind* (Netflix, 2023, produced by the Obamas' Higher Ground) was issued a **$11,617,651** NY film tax credit plus a $58,088 diversity credit, on $46.5M qualified costs and $89.0M total NY spend, per ESD's Q3 2025 quarterly report (https://esd.ny.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/Q3-Film-Report-2025.pdf, "Credits Issued" table) — appearing in the public record roughly two years after the film's release, under applicant "Netflix Productions, LLC" (Delaware).

## What didn't surprise

- The majors dominating dollars — expected; the surprise was the mechanism (naming), not the fact.
- Top-5 concentration per year (14%–45%) has no clean trend — concentration is a per-round artifact, not a story.

## Caveats (Wire standard)

- These are **allocations**, not credits ultimately claimed; projects drop out and asterisked entries (49 projects, $340M in 2025–26) are pending/conditional per CFC's list conventions — check CFC's footnote language before printing.
- All figures are the state's own published numbers. The LAO's finding that claimed economic returns are hard to verify is the book's independent-economist counterweight.
- This is one state. The same three questions run against NY ESD's quarterly reports (PDFs, machine-readable) and GA/NM/TX would either generalize the patterns or make California the outlier — both are good chapters.

## Recommendation

**Go.** One afternoon against one state's archive produced: a named-but-hidden $183M recipient, a legal carve-out that underdelivered for years and then stampeded, and a record $48M award the state disclosed only under a code name — taxpayers learned it was *Tracker* from the trades two months later, not from the state. That's a chapter one, a press hook, and a method demo — from the *easiest* archive. The book's premise ("the records are public; nobody reads them") survived contact with the records.

Next step if you want it: run the same three questions on New York's quarterly PDFs — second-biggest program, different disclosure format, and the comparison itself ("what CA tells you that NY hides, and vice versa") is a chapter.
